Silésio Carvalho • 2nd

1mo

In 2015, I went to India.

And I learned something that changed the way I looked at certifications.

Before going to India, I didn't really care that much about failing an exam. Of course, I wanted to pass but if I failed? I could always take it again. I didn't fully understand the value of the investment I was making.

While there, I talked with several teachers and professionals about certifications like CCNA, CCNP or CCIE. One thing they said really made me think: this is a big financial investment.

At first, I was surprised. But then I realized they weren't just talking about the exam fee. They were talking about the money + time + effort required to prepare for that certification.

I wasn't valuing my own investment enough. I was spending money on exams. I was spending countless hours studying. Yet mentally, I was treating failure as if it didn't really matter.

After that conversation, I started approaching certifications differently. It taught me an important lesson: the value of an investment isn't determined only by how much you spend. It's also determined by how seriously you treat it. Whether it's a certification, a degree, a course, or simply learning a new skill…

If you don't value the investment, you probably won't give it your best effort.

So, before you start your next certification, ask yourself: how much am I really investing?

A tiny seabird. 894 pieces of plastic.
A fledgling sable shearwater from Lord Howe Island was found with 894 plastic pieces in its stomach — a disturbing example of how deeply plastic pollution has entered the food chain.
What makes the finding even more worrying is the bigger pattern.
A 16-year study has linked rising plastic ingestion among Australian seabirds with poorer chick health. Young birds can mistake colourful or floating plastic for food, filling their stomachs with material that provides no nutrition while taking up space needed for real food.
The result can be starvation, weakness and reduced chances of survival.
The tragedy is that the bird doesn't understand what plastic is.
It simply eats what looks like food.
And that makes the number 894 more than a shocking statistic.
It is a reminder that pollution doesn't stay where we throw it.
It moves through oceans, ecosystems and eventually into the bodies of animals that had nothing to do with creating the problem.
We often talk about plastic as a waste-management issue.
For wildlife, it can be a survival issue.
